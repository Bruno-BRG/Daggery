Contents

Introduction

What is Duality GME?

Duality GME is an independent solo-play supplement for Daggerheart. Play without a Game Master using sworn vows, moves with clear triggers, progress tracks, and oracle tables. Your character’s Duality Dice drive the action; your imagination brings the world to life.

Your character is a normal Daggerheart character. Their traits, Experiences, Hope, Stress, Hit Points and Armor work as the core rules say, and every adversary comes from a Daggerheart stat block. What changes is who decides what happens. Instead of a GM, three things do: the moves in this book, the Fear your own rolls give the world, and a set of oracle tables.

You play by describing what your character does, making a move when the story calls for one, and interpreting the result. The dice tell you how well it went. You decide what that looks like.

What you need

  • The Daggerheart rules for character creation, combat, damage and adversaries.
  • The Duality Dice: two d12 in different colors, one for Hope and one for Fear.
  • Two d10: the challenge dice for progress rolls.
  • A d6 for advantage dice, a d20 for adversary attacks, and the damage dice your character uses.
  • A character sheet, and the tracker sheet at the back of this book (or a notebook).
  • Tokens or a counter for Hope and Fear.

Every table in this book is rolled on a d12, so the Duality Dice double as oracle dice. Play with paper and dice, a journal, a virtual tabletop, or any tools you prefer. No app or online service is required.

The core loop

  1. Create your character with the Daggerheart rules.
  2. Swear an Iron Vow on an inciting incident. This is your first quest.
  3. Begin a Session each time you sit down to play.
  4. Envision, then make moves. When you do something a move describes, make that move.
  5. Read the outcome as a strong hit, a weak hit or a miss. On a miss, Pay the Price.
  6. Mark progress on your vows, journeys, connections and scene challenges, and make a progress roll to finish them.
  7. End a Session when you stop.

That is the whole game. Everything else in this book is a detail of one of those seven steps.

Fiction first

Every move starts and ends in the story. Before you roll, envision what your character is doing and why it is risky. After you roll, envision what the result looks like. If nothing is at stake, do not roll: it simply happens. If you are not sure which move fits, choose the one closest to what your character wants. The outcome matters more than the label.

You are the GM, and you are not

Playing solo, you sit in both chairs. Keep them apart. When you decide what your character does, be bold. When you decide what the world does, be fair: take the most obvious answer, and when you would rather not choose, let the dice choose. The Fear pool, the Fear Check and the oracles exist so the world can surprise you.

How this book is organized

  • Getting Started: character, campaign and first vow.
  • Mechanics: the action roll, progress, Fear and the oracle.
  • Moves: every move, with notes on using it.
  • Oracles: every table you roll during play.
  • Example of Play and the Tracker Sheet.

Tips for solo play

  • Write short. A line per scene is enough to remember it next week.
  • Play to find out. Do not plan the plot. Plan the next scene, at most.
  • Say yes, or roll. If a thing is plausible and nothing is at stake, it happens.
  • Let your character fail. Misses make the best stories, and a price paid is a story told.

The golden rule: when the rules and the story disagree, the story wins.

If a result breaks your fun, use Change Your Fate and keep playing.

Getting Started

Character creation

Make a level 1 character with the Daggerheart rules: class and subclass, heritage, traits, Experiences, equipment and connections. A few choices matter more when you play alone:

  • Breadth beats depth. No party covers your weak spots, so a character who can fight, talk and notice things will have an easier time.
  • Experiences carry the story. Pick at least one that helps you find things out and one that helps you deal with people.
  • Hope starts as the rules say. Fear starts as the rules say for one player character.

Campaign setup

  1. Choose a tone and a place. Use a Daggerheart setting or your own. One sentence is enough: “a cold frontier where the old roads are failing.”
  2. Set your flags. Note anything you want to keep out of the story or handle with care (Set a Flag).
  3. Sketch the land. Roll Wilds for the country around you, a Settlement for where you start, and a few Place Names.
  4. Name some people. Roll Names and NPC: Role and Motive for two or three people your character knows. Any of them can become a connection.
  5. Find the inciting incident. Something has gone wrong and your character cannot ignore it. Invent it, or roll the Plot Hook tables: Objective and Opposition, then Reward and Twist.
  6. Swear an Iron Vow to set it right, and give it a rank.
  7. Begin a Session and play the first scene.

Option: a lifelong vow

Before the inciting incident, give your character a long-term vow of Dire or Legendary rank: the thing they have wanted all their life. You will rarely mark progress on it, but it explains why they keep walking, and every other vow can feed it.

Choosing a rank

Every vow, journey, connection and scene challenge has a rank. The rank says how hard it is and how long it will take. A higher rank means a higher Difficulty and slower progress.

RankA vow of this rank is…
MinorAn errand: a few scenes, a single session.
SeriousA real task: a session or two, a few obstacles.
GraveA goal: several sessions and at least one setback.
DireA quest: a long arc of the campaign.
LegendaryA life’s work: the campaign itself.

When in doubt, choose Serious for a first vow. It is long enough to matter and short enough to finish.

Writing a good vow

A vow is a promise your character makes out loud. Make it personal (why does it matter to them?), concrete (how will you know it is done?) and urgent (what happens if they fail?).

Weak: “Deal with the bandits.”
Strong: “I will bring back the miller’s daughter before the bandits cross the river.”

Your first session

Start in motion. Put your character close to the trouble of the inciting incident, already doing something. Make your first move within a few minutes of play. You will learn the rest of the world by playing through it.

Sample vows

RankVow
Minor“I will get the ferryman’s boat back before dawn.”
Serious“I will find who poisoned the well and bring them to the elders.”
Grave“I will drive the wyrm from the old mine.”
Dire“I will unite the river clans before the war comes.”
Legendary“I will restore my fallen house to its seat.”

Quick start

1. Make a Daggerheart character.

2. Roll a Plot Hook, or invent trouble.

3. Swear an Iron Vow (Serious).

4. Begin a Session. Envision. Make moves.

Mechanics Summary

Moves

A move is a rule for a moment of the story. Each one has a trigger (“When you…”), usually a roll, and outcomes. When your character does what a trigger describes, make the move and follow its instructions. When this book names a move, it is written in italics.

The action roll

Most moves call for a Daggerheart action roll. Roll the Duality Dice, add both, then add the trait the move names and any modifiers (an Experience, an advantage die). Compare the total to the Difficulty. Equal or higher is a success.

Then look at the dice: if the Hope die is higher, you rolled with Hope; if the Fear die is higher, you rolled with Fear. If they match, it is a critical success.

Reading the outcome

Every action roll becomes one of three move outcomes:

✓✓

Strong hit: a success with Hope, or a critical success. You do it, cleanly.

✓✗

Weak hit: a success with Fear. You do it, but at a cost or with less than you wanted.

✗✗

Miss: a failure, with Hope or with Fear. You fail, or the situation gets worse. Pay the Price.

The dice still do what Daggerheart says: a roll with Hope gives you a Hope, a roll with Fear gives the world a Fear, and a critical success gives you a Hope and clears a Stress.

A failure with Fear is the worst result in the game. The world gains a Fear, and it may spend 1 Fear at once to make the price severe.

Hope and Momentum

Hope is Daggerheart’s. You gain it on rolls with Hope and on a critical success. Spend it exactly as the Daggerheart rules allow: to add an Experience, to power your class’s Hope feature, and so on. Hope keeps its usual maximum.

Momentum is Duality GME’s own, and it comes from Ironsworn. It says how well your quest is going, from −6 to +10. Moves tell you when to take it and when to suffer it, and you can burn it to turn a roll your way (page ).

Hope is your answer to bad luck. A miss you can turn into a hit with an Experience is not a miss yet: decide before you read the outcome. Momentum is your answer to a bad roll: decide after.

Fear is the world’s

There is no GM to hold Fear, but Fear still exists. It grows when you roll with Fear and when you rest, as the rules say, up to 12. The world never invents Fear: it only spends what your rolls give it. It spends Fear through the Fear Check, on severe prices, and in combat on the adversaries’ Fear features.

Advantage

Some moves give you advantage on your next roll. As in Daggerheart, roll a d6 advantage die and add it to that roll.

Three rolls, three outcomes

Wren leaps a crevasse: Face Danger, Agility +2, Difficulty 13.

Hope 8, Fear 5: 15, with Hope. A strong hit: she lands cleanly. She gains a Hope for rolling with Hope, and takes +1 momentum.

Hope 4, Fear 9: 15, with Fear. A weak hit: she makes it, but marks a Stress as the edge crumbles. The world gains a Fear.

Hope 3, Fear 6: 11, a failure with Fear. A miss: she falls short and must Pay the Price. The world gains a Fear, and may spend it at once to make the price severe.

Set the Difficulty

When an action calls for a roll, set the Difficulty before rolling. Use facts already established in the scene, not the number you hope to beat.

1. Use an existing value first

  1. If the rule or move specifies a Difficulty, use it.
  2. If you are overcoming an adversary or NPC with a defined Difficulty, use theirs.
  3. If the move tells you to use a challenge's rank, use that rank's Difficulty.

Do not add obstacle increases to these values. Only use the procedure below when none of these sources provides the Difficulty. A nearby progress track does not automatically set every action's Difficulty; the move must call for its rank.

2. Otherwise, start at 13

13 is the default for an uncertain action. You do not need to justify why it is not harder. Check for three distinct obstacles:

ObstacleWhat must already be true?
ResistanceA relevant safeguard or committed opposition stands in your way: a reinforced lock, guards on alert, or someone under explicit orders to refuse.
DemandThe result requires unusual precision, reach or personal concession: a tiny mechanism, an exceptional gap, or a request that risks someone's position.
ConditionsSomething concretely hinders the attempt: darkness without a light, unstable footing, or a crowd preventing conversation.
Distinct obstaclesDifficulty
None13
One15
Two18
Three20

Count each category at most once. Count the same fact only once, even if it could fit two categories. Ordinary uncertainty is already part of 13: a normal lock is not automatically a Resistance obstacle. Do not invent obstacles to reach a preferred number. If no established fact supports an obstacle, do not count it.

3. Easy, certain or impossible?

  • Difficulty 10: no obstacles, and a concrete fact makes the task especially simple, but uncertainty remains.
  • No roll: success is evident, or failure would change nothing.
  • Impossible right now: do not roll; first change the situation.

Judge the task, not the hero. Traits and Experiences already modify the roll; do not count them again when setting Difficulty. Do not also use the same fact as an obstacle increase and as disadvantage, or as both the reason for Difficulty 10 and an advantage die. Separate facts may affect the roll separately.

Example: convincing a guard

If the guard has a defined Difficulty, use it. Otherwise:

  • Asking a willing person for ordinary information may need no roll.
  • Convincing an undecided person to help with a reasonable task: 13.
  • Convincing a guard explicitly ordered to deny entry: Resistance, 15.
  • Asking that guard to abandon their post and risk their job: Resistance + Demand, 18.
  • Making that request amid a crowd that prevents conversation: all three, 20.

Lock it before rolling

Say the number and the reason: "Difficulty 15: the guard has orders to refuse." Do not change it after seeing the dice. Reassess only when the situation changes.

Danger is not Difficulty. A simple crossing can still have a deadly fall. Danger changes the consequence of failure; it does not automatically raise the number. This is a Duality GME procedure for consistent rulings, not a guarantee that every action has the same chance of success.

Progress and Clocks

Progress tracks

Your goals are measured on progress tracks. Vows, journeys, connections and scene challenges each get one. A track has 10 boxes, and each box holds 4 ticks, so a full track is 40 ticks. Draw the ticks as you earn them:

One to four ticks in a box. Four ticks make a full box.

Marking progress

When a move tells you to mark progress, fill the track by an amount set by its rank:

So a Minor track fills in 4 marks, a Serious one in 5, a Grave one in 10, a Dire one in 20 and a Legendary one in 40. You will rarely fill a Dire or Legendary track; you roll long before that.

A track with 20 ticks: a progress score of 5.

The progress roll

When a move asks for a progress roll, count the full boxes on the track: that is your progress score, from 0 to 10. Partly filled boxes do not count. Roll the two challenge dice (d10) and compare your score to each.

Progress rolls never give Hope or Fear, and no modifier, Experience or Hope can change them. Your progress is your bonus.

Matches

When both challenge dice show the same number, it is a match. Something unexpected happens. On a hit, the twist is in your favor: a new opportunity, a revelation, an extra reward. On a miss, it is a dire turn: a new danger, a betrayal, a worse outcome than the move says. If you are unsure what the twist is, Ask the Oracle.

When to roll

You decide when to make a progress roll, as long as the story allows it. A full track is not required. Rolling early is a gamble: a score of 4 beats a d10 less than half the time. Rolling late costs time and scenes, and the world keeps moving. That tension is the heart of the game.

Tension clocks

A clock measures a danger that is coming: guards closing in, a storm, a ritual nearing completion. It has 4, 6 or 8 segments. When something makes the danger worse, fill a segment. When the clock is full, the danger arrives.

4: at a disadvantage
6: even footing
8: with an advantage

Clocks appear in scene challenges, but you can start one whenever a threat should grow over time. The Pay the Price table can tick one, and so can the Vignette at the start of a session.

Progress roll odds

How likely each outcome is for each progress score. Use it to decide whether to roll now or push for one more box.

Momentum

Momentum is how well your quest is going. It is your side of the table, as Fear is the world’s: Fear is what the world has to spend against you, and Momentum is what you have to spend for yourself. It runs from −6 to +10 and starts at +2. Keep it on your tracker sheet.

Momentum comes from Ironsworn. Where Ironsworn compares your action score to two challenge dice, Duality GME compares the Duality Dice, so the rules for burning it are written for the Hope and Fear dice.

Take and suffer

A move that says take +2 momentum adds 2, up to your maximum. A move that says suffer −1 momentum subtracts 1, down to −6. Strong hits usually give Momentum. Weak hits give a little, or cost some. Misses make you suffer, or you Pay the Price instead.

If Momentum is already at −6 and you must suffer more, make the move Face a Setback (page ).

Burn Momentum

After an action roll, and before you play out the result, you may burn your Momentum if it is higher than your Fear die. When you do:

  • Ignore the Fear die. The result improves by one step: a miss becomes a weak hit, and a weak hit becomes a strong hit.
  • The world does not gain Fear from this roll.
  • Your Momentum resets (see below).

A strong hit, a critical success or a progress roll cannot be improved, so there is nothing to burn. You cannot burn Momentum on a progress roll, and it does not hurt you there either.

Reset and maximum

Each marked debility (page ) lowers your maximum Momentum by 1 and your reset.

Negative Momentum

When Momentum is below 0 and its number matches your Hope die, cancel that die. Take its number off the total, and the roll cannot be a roll with Hope. Progress rolls are not affected.

Example: burning Momentum

Wren has Momentum +6 and rolls Face Danger. Hope 4, Fear 5, Agility +2: 11 against Difficulty 13, a failure with Fear. Her Momentum, 6, is higher than the Fear die, 5, so she may burn it. The Fear die is ignored: the miss becomes a weak hit. She gets across, and the world does not gain a Fear. Her Momentum resets to +2.

Example: a poor roll

Wren has Momentum −3. She rolls a 3 on the Hope die. It matches, so she cancels it and takes 3 off her total. A good reason to Secure an Advantage and take +1 momentum before she acts.

Burn Momentum when the result matters, not when it is easy. A high Momentum is a safety net. Spending it too early leaves you without one when the real danger comes.

Trackers

Hit Points and Stress

Ironsworn’s health and spirit are the Hit Points and Stress on your Daggerheart sheet. “Suffer −2 health” means mark 2 Hit Points. “Take +1 health” means clear 1 Hit Point. “Suffer −1 spirit” means mark 1 Stress, and “take +1 spirit” means clear 1. When you cannot mark any more, a move tells you what to suffer instead.

Supply

Supply stands for your food, gear and preparation. It runs from 0 to 5 and starts at 5. A move that says suffer −1 supply takes a point. When Supply falls to 0, mark Unprepared. Any more Supply you must suffer while Unprepared is paid with Hit Points, Stress or Momentum: that is the move Out of Supply.

Conditions and debilities

Conditions live on your character sheet, not on the tracker. Daggerheart has three: Hidden (rolls against you have disadvantage), Restrained (you cannot move) and Vulnerable (rolls that target you have advantage). Ironsworn adds eight debilities. Each debility lowers your maximum Momentum and your reset by 1.

Initiative

Initiative is the spotlight in a fight: who is acting, and who is reacting. It is the same idea as Daggerheart’s spotlight.

  • You have initiative: you are in control. Make proactive moves: Strike, Secure an Advantage, Face Danger, Aid Your Ally, Compel or End the Fight.
  • Your foe has initiative: they act and you react. Make reactive moves: Clash, Face Danger, Compel or Turn the Tide.

A strong hit on a combat move takes or keeps initiative. A weak hit or a miss passes it to your foe. Once per fight, Turn the Tide steals it back. When no fight is on, nobody has initiative: leave the tracker blank.

Milestones and levels

There is no experience count. As in Daggerheart, you level up at a milestone: you Fulfill Your Vow of rank Grave, Dire or Legendary, you finish a story arc, or the table agrees it is time. Then use Advance: follow the level-up steps on your character sheet. One milestone gives one level. Minor and Serious vows give Hope and Stress relief instead.

Momentum of a fight

Every fight has its own record. When the fight begins, write down your start Momentum. Note the best and the worst it reaches. When the fight ends, write the net: the end minus the start. The app does this for you. Your Momentum is still one number for the whole character: the record only shows how each fight went.

Ironsworn stats and Duality traits

Ironsworn has five stats where Daggerheart has six traits. Use the trait that matches how you act.

A +1 on a roll in Ironsworn is +2 here, because the Duality Dice add up to more than a d6 and a stat.

Three things to keep on the table: Momentum, Supply and Fear. Momentum is yours, Fear is the world’s, and Supply is what stands between you and both.

The World’s Voice

Three tools replace the GM: the Fear Check decides when the world acts, Pay the Price decides what a miss costs, and the oracle answers your questions. Learn these three and you can run any situation.

The Fear Check

Roll a d12. If the result is equal to or under the current Fear, the check passes: the world spends Fear and acts. Otherwise it fails. At 0 Fear it always fails.

The more Fear you have given the world, the more often it acts. A run of rolls with Fear makes everything more dangerous; resting, which adds Fear, is never free.

Make a Fear Check when a move or table tells you to:

  • in a fight, each time the spotlight passes to the GM (The GM Turn);
  • on a failure with Fear while you Explore a Waypoint;
  • on each leg of a Journey;
  • and, if you want the dice to decide, on a failure with Fear, to see whether the world spends 1 Fear for a severe price.

Pay the Price

On a miss, something goes wrong. Choose one way to find out what:

  • Make the most obvious negative outcome happen. If one leaps to mind, use it.
  • Ask the Oracle between two outcomes: rate one as Likely and roll.
  • Roll on the Pay the Price table and fit the result to the scene.

A severe price comes from a failure with Fear when the world spends 1 Fear: roll twice, and both happen. A 12 on the table also means two prices.

A price should change the situation, not just hurt. Take the damage, yes, but also lose the trail, break the rope, or let the guard see you.

Ask the Oracle

When you have a question the story cannot answer, ask the oracle. Phrase it as a yes or no question, pick how likely a yes is, and roll the Duality Dice. Add them: a yes needs the total shown.

If the dice match (doubles), the answer comes with an extreme result or a twist: “yes, and more than you hoped” or “no, and worse.” Oracle rolls never give Hope or Fear.

Meaning

For questions a yes or no cannot answer (“What does the stranger want?”), roll Meaning. Roll both Duality Dice for each word: the Hope die picks the row and the Fear die picks the column (odd: A, even: B). Roll Action + Subject for events and intentions, Descriptor + Subject for things and places.

Interpreting results

  • Take the first reading that makes sense in the scene. Do not hunt for the perfect one.
  • If it does not fit, look at the row above or below, or roll again.
  • Keep it small. A table result is a seed, not a plot. “A new danger appears” can be a dog barking.
  • Ask the next question. One answer usually suggests the next question.

Making Moves

Move glossary

Advantage. Add a d6 advantage die to the roll.

Twist. A result that turns the story: from doubles on the oracle, or a match on the challenge dice.

Rank. Minor, Serious, Grave, Dire or Legendary.

Fear Check. A d12 equal to or under Fear passes (page ).

Using moves well

Trigger, then roll. Only make a move when its trigger happens in the story. If you want to know whether something is possible, ask the oracle first.

One roll, one outcome. Do not reroll a move because you dislike the result. Use Hope, Experiences, or Change Your Fate instead.

Not every action is a move. Opening a door, buying bread and walking a safe road just happen.

Index of Moves

Session Moves

Session moves frame your time at the table. They keep the story coherent from one sitting to the next and let you steer away from content you do not want. They are Duality GME’s own: Ironsworn has no session moves.

Quest Moves

Quests drive the story. Each vow is a promise with a progress track. You swear it, reach milestones toward it, and at last fulfill it or let it go. A Grave or greater vow is a milestone: see Advance.

Adventure Moves

These are the moves you make most. When in doubt, Face Danger to get through something and Secure an Advantage to set up the next roll.

Camp and Journey Moves

A journey is travel through dangerous ground: a haunted forest, a mountain pass, the ruined quarter of a city. It has a rank and a progress track. You travel in segments, and each one brings you closer and may bring you to a waypoint worth exploring.

The shape of a journey

  1. Undertake a Journey: name it, rank it, and roll for the first segment.
  2. On a hit, mark progress and reach a waypoint. Explore a Waypoint if it is worth a look.
  3. Keep rolling segments until you feel ready.
  4. Reach Your Destination with a progress roll.

Rest and recovery

Make Camp is your rest in the wild. In a town or a safe house, use Sojourn. A Daggerheart short rest or long rest also works as the rules say: it clears Hit Points and Stress, repairs armor, and gives the world Fear. Roll Rest and Camp after a camp if you want a surprise.

Ranking a journey

RankThe journey is…
MinorA moderate distance in one region.
SeriousA long way in one region, or across rough ground.
GraveFrom one region to another, or especially hard terrain.
DireThrough several regions.
LegendaryFrom one end of the land to the other, or beyond.

The rank sets the Difficulty of each segment roll and how fast you mark progress. A Minor trip takes about four good segments; a Grave one, ten.

Exploration Moves

These moves are Duality GME’s own. They make a waypoint into a scene of its own: something to find, something to fear, something to wonder at.

Journeys and the road

Not every trip is a journey. For an ordinary road, use the Journeys table instead: a Fear Check for each leg, and a Danger or a Discovery. Save Undertake a Journey for travel that is a story in itself, where reaching the place is the challenge.

Waypoints worth a stop

Explore a waypoint when it could hold what you need: shelter, a clue, a way around. Skip it when you are in a hurry. A good waypoint has a feature (what you see), a danger (what could go wrong) and a question (why it is here).

Example: Gather Information

Wren asks the ferryman about the missing healer. She rolls Instinct and gets a weak hit: he saw a boat leave at night, but the man at the oars wore the colors of Ashford’s own guard. Wren learns something true and the story gets more complicated. That is what a weak hit should do.

Costs of a weak hit

A weak hit is a success with strings attached. When a move says “at a cost,” pick the one that fits the scene, or the one that hurts the story most without stopping it:

  • Momentum: suffer −1 momentum. It is the default cost.
  • Stress: mark a Stress, or Endure Stress.
  • Time: it takes longer; a clock fills or a rival gains ground.
  • Position: you are exposed, separated or off balance.
  • Resources: you use up a consumable, damage a tool, or spend coin.
  • Less: you get only part of what you wanted.

Relationship Moves

Relationship moves are how you deal with other people: persuade them, rest among them, face them in a duel, and bind yourself to them. A bond is a lasting tie to a person or a community. Mark one tick on your bonds track for each. While you share a bond, many moves give you +2.

Bonds

A connection is someone who matters to your character: a guide, a rival, a merchant, a friend. Give each a role. When the time is right, Forge a Bond with them.

Scene Challenge

Scene challenge

A scene challenge is for a tense situation that takes more than one roll: a chase through the market, sneaking into a keep, a debate before the council. You race a grave progress track (your objective) against a tension clock (the danger).

Example: the chase

A thief snatches the map. Your objective: get the map back. You are on foot in a crowd he knows, so the clock has 4 segments. You Face Danger with Agility to vault a cart (strong hit: mark progress), then Secure an Advantage with Instinct to cut through an alley (weak hit: mark progress and fill a segment). With 2 boxes marked and a quarter of the clock gone, you decide to Finish the Scene now rather than risk losing him.

Foes

Every foe has a rank, from Minor to Legendary. The rank says how hard the foe hits you (its harm) and how fast your blows wear it down (the progress each of your harm marks on its track). It comes from Ironsworn. Daggerheart gives every adversary a tier and a type, so this page turns one into the other.

From stat block to rank

Pick a Daggerheart adversary, then read its rank: rank = tier − 1 + the role bonus, from Minor (0) to Legendary (4).

The stat block still gives you the foe’s features, attacks and Fear moves. The rank gives you its health: a track of ten boxes instead of Hit Points.

No stat block yet?

Roll Challenge Rank (Foes) on the d100 oracle for a rank at random (page ), then roll Adversary: Creature and Situation and Adversary Type to find what it is. In the app, Foe rank does both and suggests stat blocks.

Packs

You may fight a group of Minor or Serious foes as one pack, with a single track. A small pack (about 3 to 5) is one rank higher. A large pack (about 6 to 10) is two ranks higher. Do not make packs of Grave foes or higher: fight them one by one.

Harm

Your harm. With a deadly weapon (a sword, an axe, a spear, a bow, a Daggerheart weapon) you inflict 2 harm. Unarmed, or with a simple weapon (a shield, a staff, a rock), you inflict 1 harm. Some moves add +1.

Each harm marks progress on the foe’s track by its rank. Two harm against a Serious foe marks 4 boxes.

Their harm. When a foe hurts you, Endure Harm by its rank: a Minor foe marks 1 Hit Point, a Legendary foe 5. If you have an armor slot, you may mark it to take 1 less, as Daggerheart armor does.

Example: a hunter and his hound

The bounty hunter is a tier 1 Standard: Minor. His hound is a tier 1 Minion: Minor. Each gets its own track. Wren’s arrow inflicts 2 harm: 6 boxes on the hunter’s track. If he hits her, she marks 1 Hit Point.

Start with the foes you know. If a fight is too slow, raise their rank. If it is too fast, lower it. Ranks are a dial, not a law.

Combat Moves

Duality GME runs a fight the way Ironsworn does, on initiative and Momentum, and keeps Daggerheart for what the foes can do. Your attacks, damage, thresholds and armor work as the rules say. Adversaries use their stat blocks, and each has a rank and a progress track (page ).

Combat Moves continued

Running a fight

  1. Choose your foes. Use the adversaries the story calls for, or roll Foe rank, Creature and Situation and Adversary Type. Give each foe or pack a rank and a track.
  2. Enter the Fray to see who has initiative.
  3. With initiative you act: Strike, Secure an Advantage, Face Danger, Aid Your Ally or Compel.
  4. Without it your foe acts, then you react: Clash, Face Danger, Compel, or Turn the Tide once.
  5. Keep or lose the lead. A strong hit takes or keeps initiative. A weak hit or a miss gives it to your foe.
  6. End the Fight when you take decisive action and score a strong hit, with a progress roll on the foe’s track.

Running Fights

When your foe has initiative

Your foe acts. Pick what makes the most sense, or let the oracle decide. Choose one:

  • Combat Action. Roll the d100 oracle for what the foe does (page ).
  • The GM turn. Make a Fear Check. On a fail the foe acts for free (roll Tactics). On a pass, roll Fear Spend and pay the cost. Then check again after each action: a pass spends 1 Fear and brings in another adversary. The first fail ends the turn.

Then you react. If the foe hurt you, Endure Harm by its rank. Daggerheart’s Fear features, Fear moves and thresholds can all be used for the foe’s abilities; the track is its health.

Playing the adversaries

  • Give them a goal. Few foes fight to the death. Decide what each one wants: the loot, the door, your surrender.
  • Use their features. When Fear Spend calls for a feature, pick the one that fits the moment best.
  • Let them break. When a fight turns against them, Ask the Oracle whether they run (Likely for minions, Unlikely for solos).

A Hard Move result on the Fear Spend table means the world strikes hard: treat it as a severe Pay the Price.

Fleeing and surrender

You can always try to leave a fight. Breaking away under attack is Face Danger; a long pursuit is a scene challenge with your escape as the objective. To make a foe give up, Compel. If you are beaten you are not always dead: Ask the Oracle whether they take you prisoner, and let a new vow begin there.

Example: the GM turn

Wren misses a Clash against the bounty hunter, and he has initiative. Fear is 4. The Fear Check rolls a 2: it passes. Fear Spend rolls the hunter’s Fear feature; the world pays 1 Fear (Fear 3) and the hunter lunges. The world checks again: a 3, another pass. It spends 1 Fear (Fear 2) and the hunter’s hound joins in, rolling Tactics. The third check is a 9, which fails, and the GM turn ends. The spotlight returns to Wren.

Fight a full battle when the fight is the scene. Battle when it is in the way of the scene.

Suffer Moves

Suffer moves answer harm. You make them when a foe, a hazard or a bad price hurts you. They use the harm or stress of the foe’s rank: Minor 1, Serious 2, Grave 3, Dire 4, Legendary 5.

Fate Moves

Fate moves are how the world speaks. Other moves send you here: a miss tells you to Pay the Price, and any uncertainty is a reason to Ask the Oracle.

Ask the Oracle

Roll the Duality Dice and add them. Doubles: an extreme result or a twist.

Choosing the likelihood

Pick the likelihood from what your character knows, not what you hope. If the answer would be strange, it is Unlikely. If the scene points toward it, it is Likely. If you truly have no idea, it is 50 / 50. Use the extremes for things that are nearly settled but could still surprise you.

Questions worth asking

Is anyone here? Is it guarded? Do they believe me? Is it what it seems? Does it get worse? Is there another way?

Pay the Price

Roll a d12. Fit the result to the scene; if it makes no sense, take the next row.

Ironsworn’s own d100 table is on page . Use either one.

Prices by situation

In a fight: damage, a lost weapon, a new foe, an ally down.
On the road: lost time, lost supplies, a wrong turn, bad weather.
Among people: a lost friend, a broken promise, a rumor about you.
On a vow: lose progress, the goal moves, a rival gets there first.

Example: a severe price

Wren fails to cross a frozen river with Fear. The world spends 1 Fear and the price is severe: two rolls on the table. “Something of value is lost” and “Time is lost.” Her pack goes under the ice, and by the time she is warm again the trail is a day old.

Meaning Oracles

Roll both Duality Dice for each word. The Hope die picks the row; the Fear die picks the column: odd for A, even for B. Combine Action + Subject for what someone wants or what happens, and Descriptor + Subject for what something is.

Read the pair as loosely as you need. “Betray + Home” could be a traitor in your village, a house that is not what it seems, or an exile’s longing.

Rumors and Leads

From hook to vow

A Plot Hook is raw material. Turn it into a vow by asking three questions: Why does my character care? Who asked them, or who will suffer? How will they know it is done? Then write the vow in your character’s voice and give it a rank.

Keep the Twist secret, even from yourself if you can: write it on a slip of paper and read it when a match, a doubles or a Fulfill Your Vow calls for a turn.

Picking a stat block

Use adversaries of your character’s tier: tier 1 at level 1, tier 2 at levels 2 to 4, tier 3 at levels 5 to 7, tier 4 at levels 8 to 10. Read the type rolled above as a role:

  • Minions and hordes come in numbers and fall fast.
  • Standard foes are the backbone of a fight.
  • Bruisers hit hard; ranged foes keep their distance; skulks strike from hiding.
  • Leaders and supports make the others more dangerous.
  • Social adversaries fight with words.
  • A solo is a whole fight by itself.

Example of Play

Wren is a level 1 ranger: Agility +2, Instinct +1, Presence +1, Finesse 0, Knowledge 0, Strength −1. She starts with 2 Hope and Momentum +2; the world starts with 1 Fear.

The inciting incident

The well at Ashford has been poisoned, and the only healer who could cure it, old Maro, has vanished. Wren grew up drinking from that well. She swears a vow: “I will find Maro and cure Ashford’s well.” It is a real task, so the vow is Serious (Difficulty 13).

Swear an Iron Vow, Presence: the Hope die shows 9, the Fear die 4. 9 + 4 + 1 = 14 against 13, and Hope is higher: a strong hit. Wren gains a Hope for rolling with Hope and takes +2 momentum from the move. She knows what to do next: search Maro’s house. Hope 3, Fear 1, Momentum +4.

Begin a Session

The next evening, Wren’s player begins a session and rolls a Vignette: a 2, “A rival gains ground on your vow.” Far away, a bounty hunter is asking about Maro too. Wren does not know, but the player does. Wren takes +1 momentum as the story returns to her. Momentum +5.

Gather Information

At Maro’s house, Wren searches with Instinct against Difficulty 12. Hope 3, Fear 8: 3 + 8 + 1 = 12, a success, but with Fear. A weak hit, and the world gains a Fear. Wren learns something, but it complicates things, and she takes +1 momentum. Momentum +6.

The player asks the oracle, “Did Maro leave willingly?” at 50 / 50 (13+). Hope 5, Fear 5: a total of 10, so no. The dice match, so there is a twist: Maro was taken by someone she trusted. A teacup sits across from hers, still full. Fear 2.

Into the Gloamwood

The trail leads into the forest. Wren Undertakes a Journey, Serious, with Instinct: Hope 10, Fear 6, total 17. A strong hit: she reaches a waypoint, and chooses to make good use of her resources: she marks progress (2 boxes). Hope 4.

The Waypoint roll is a 9: a strange monolith. Wren looks closer with Knowledge: Hope 4, Fear 7, total 11. A failure with Fear: a miss, and Fear rises to 3. Her Momentum, +6, is not higher than the Fear die, 7, so she cannot burn it.

A miss on Explore a Waypoint is a peril, so Wren must Pay the Price. The failure with Fear also calls for a Fear Check: the d12 shows 3, equal to the Fear. It passes. The world spends 1 Fear (Fear 2) and Wren must Confront Chaos: a 3, “A voice speaks your name.” The stone whispers “Wren” in Maro’s voice. For the price, the player rolls a 5: Mark a Stress.

Wren tries to tear herself away: Face Danger with Agility. Hope 7, Fear 7: doubles, a critical success. She is free, and the critical gives a Hope (Hope 5), clears a Stress, and a strong hit takes +1 momentum. Momentum +7.

The end of the vow

Over the next sessions Wren follows the voice, reaches three milestones and fills 6 boxes on her vow. In a hidden glade she finds Maro, alive, and the bounty hunter standing over her. After the fight, Wren makes the progress roll for Fulfill Your Vow: the challenge dice show 4 and 8. A score of 6 beats one: a weak hit. Maro is saved and the well can be cured, but whoever betrayed her is still out there. She marks 1 experience. Momentum does not count on a progress roll. Wren swears a new vow.

A closer look: the fight in the glade

The bounty hunter is the scene, so Wren fights a full battle. The player picks a tier 1 Standard adversary for the hunter and a tier 1 Minion for his hound. Both are Minor foes (page ), so each gets a track, and each deals 1 harm. Fear is 4. Momentum is +7.

Enter the Fray, Instinct (the hound catches her scent): Hope 6, Fear 9, total 16. A success with Fear: a weak hit. The world gains a Fear (Fear 5). Wren chooses to bolster her position and takes +2 momentum (+9). She does not take the initiative, so the hunter has it.

The hunter acts. The Fear Check rolls a 7: more than 5, so it fails. The hunter acts for free: Tactics. He circles to put Maro between himself and Wren’s bow. After that action the world checks again to chain: a 10, another fail, so his turn ends. Wren reacts.

Clash. Wren shoots at the advancing hound with Agility: Hope 8, Fear 3, total 13. A strong hit: she inflicts 2 harm, which is 6 boxes on the hound’s track, and she takes initiative. She also bolsters her position: +1 momentum (+10).

Strike. With initiative, she shoots the hunter: Hope 4, Fear 9, total 15. A weak hit. She inflicts 2 harm: 6 boxes on his track. The world gains a Fear (Fear 6), and the hunter takes initiative. He lunges and hurts her: Endure Harm, 1 harm. She marks a Hit Point and rolls Strength: a weak hit, so she presses on.

End the Fight. A few moves later Wren scores a strong hit on a decisive shot. The hunter’s track is full, so she makes a progress roll with a score of 10: the dice show 7 and 3. A strong hit: he yields. Wren asks the oracle, “Does he know who hired him?” Likely: yes. That answer is her next vow.

Quick Reference

The action roll

Ranks

No specified Difficulty? Start at 13. Count Resistance, Demand and Conditions once each: 0 / 1 / 2 / 3 obstacles gives 13 / 15 / 18 / 20. No obstacles and especially simple: 10. Lock it before rolling. See page .

Progress roll

Full boxes vs two d10. Beat both: strong hit. Beat one: weak hit. Beat neither: miss. Ties go to the dice. A match is a twist. No Hope, no Fear, no modifiers.

Fear Check

d12 equal to or under Fear: passes, the world spends Fear and acts. At 0 Fear it always fails.

Momentum

−6 to +10, starts at +2. Burn it after an action roll when it is higher than your Fear die: ignore the Fear die, improve the result one step, no Fear for the world, then reset (+2, +1 with one debility, 0 with more). Negative Momentum that matches your Hope die cancels that die.

Fights

Enter the Fray: strong hit, +2 momentum and initiative. Strong hits take or keep initiative; weak hits and misses lose it. Strike with initiative, Clash without. End the Fight with a progress roll. Foe harm is its rank, 1 to 5. Each harm you inflict marks its rank.

Ask the Oracle

Doubles: an extreme result or a twist. Open questions: roll Meaning.

Pay the Price

The obvious outcome, the oracle between two, or the table. Severe: roll twice.

Tension clock

4 segments at a disadvantage, 6 on even footing, 8 with an advantage.
